# US midterms: what markets and polls expect, and what each result could mean

*As of 23:20 UTC on 2 November 2026 (6:20 p.m. ET). This is general market analysis, not personal investment advice.*

Americans vote on Tuesday 3 November for all 435 House seats, 35 Senate seats and most governors. Republicans hold both chambers: Democrats need a net gain of four Senate seats (the split is 53-47) and three House seats (Election Central, 2 November).

## What is expected

Prediction markets strongly favour Democrats to win the House. They also clearly favour Democrats to take the Senate, though less decisively. Among the four possible combinations, a Democratic sweep of both chambers leads by a wide margin. A Democratic House with a Republican Senate is the main alternative. Republicans keeping both chambers is an outside chance, and a Republican House with a Democratic Senate is remote. Markets moved further towards Democrats in the final days.

Polls point the same way. The Silver Bulletin generic-ballot average gives Democrats an 8.9-point lead (updated 2 November). PollCheck has Democrats at 51.1% and Republicans at 42.8%, an 8.3-point lead (updated 2 November). Some are less sure about the Senate: Wolfe Research's own estimate put Democratic control at 49% (CNBC, 30 October).

## When we will know

The first polls close at 23:00 UTC (6 p.m. ET). Most Senate battlegrounds close between 00:30 and 02:00 UTC, California at 04:00 UTC and the last of Alaska at 06:00 UTC (270toWin). In 2018, networks called the House at roughly 10 p.m. ET. Some races could take far longer: Alaska expects final ranked-choice results by 18 November (Newsweek), Maine has usually known a ranked-choice result the week after, and California counties have about two weeks to report most ballots (PolitiFact via PBS). If the Senate comes down to Alaska or Maine, control could stay unknown for days.

## How much the vote is priced

Not much, by options standards. On 29 October, S&P 500 options priced a move of about 1.3% in either direction by Wednesday's close, which Saxo described as little more than an ordinary day. Since 1990 the index has usually moved less than 1% the day after a midterm, but about 2% after both 2018 and 2022 (Saxo, 30 October).

The backdrop may matter as much as the vote. The Federal Reserve held rates at 3.75-4.00% on 28 October, with two officials wanting a hike. Futures lean towards a December rise (Yahoo Finance live coverage, 2 November). The 2-year Treasury yield closed 2 November at 4.59% and the 10-year at 4.91% (US Treasury). Oil fell sharply on 2 November on hopes of Middle East diplomacy.

## What each outcome could mean

**Democratic sweep (the expected result).** The President's veto means tax and tariff policy is unlikely to change. Expect more oversight, a harder funding fight before the 11 December deadline, and a debt-limit standoff in 2027. Since this result is widely expected, moves may be modest, and strategists disagree on stocks: Ed Yardeni warns of a "very cold shower", while Morgan Stanley's Mike Wilson sees a possible clearing event. Less fiscal expansion could ease long-term yields and soften the dollar a little, helping gold at the margin. Man Group and Saxo see healthcare, retail and parts of clean energy benefiting, and banks and brokers, defence and AI data-centre builders under more pressure. Oil would take its cue from the Middle East, not Congress.

**Democratic House, Republican Senate.** Gridlock, which markets have often welcomed. The Senate would keep its power to confirm nominees, including at the Fed. Against an expected sweep, this is a modest surprise. Stocks could firm, the dollar could edge up and long-term yields could rise slightly, while the sector winners and losers of a sweep partly swap places.

**Republican sweep.** The biggest surprise on offer. It would reopen the door to tax and spending legislation and easier permits for energy and data centres. Stocks could rally, led by financials, energy, defence and AI infrastructure. Larger deficits would probably lift long-term yields and the dollar, and gold could come under pressure. Bitcoin would likely benefit from a crypto-friendly Congress, while insurers and hospitals that rely on public health programmes, and clean energy, would face headwinds. After the 2024 presidential result the dollar and yields jumped and gold fell: a reference point, not a forecast.

**Republican House, Democratic Senate.** Remote, with no clear market template.

**A delayed or contested result.** A slow count would keep uncertainty priced for longer. A formal dispute over a chamber-deciding result would be a different kind of risk, one the scenarios above do not cover.

## What would change the view

- Early results in Virginia, Georgia, North Carolina, Ohio and Florida (00:00-00:30 UTC), compared with the polls.
- The Senate tipping points: Texas, Maine, Ohio and Alaska, with Kansas and Iowa as Democrats' extra chances.
- Whether stocks move by much more than the roughly 1.3% that options priced, which would mean the result surprised.
- The Middle East and oil, the jobs report on 6 November and inflation data on 10 November, any of which could outweigh the election in rates and the dollar.

*Sources: Election Central; Silver Bulletin; PollCheck; CNBC; 270toWin; Newsweek; PolitiFact/PBS; Saxo; Benzinga/Yahoo Finance; Federal Reserve; US Treasury. Prediction-market readings are described in words only.*
