We think a trading rule earns attention only when its costs, direction and limits travel with its historical record.

Our measured-odds series starts with that record's construction. A profitable-looking row can reflect a rising market, pooled directions or a generous assumption about execution.

This page explains the method behind the series. It describes hypothetical historical trades, rather than Capital Chronicle's own trades or a forecast for the next one.

## What was counted

We compared 54 market-and-behaviour rows in the original table. The sample spans November 2016 to November 2026.

Each later piece gives the number of trades, its period and the rule being tested. A count without its denominator hides how much evidence supports it.

The rules were fixed before their historical trades were counted. That does not make the whole research exercise a test conducted before those years unfolded.

## How the trend yardstick works

The yardstick calls a market up only when price rises over 20 and 60 sessions. Price must also exceed its 20-session and 50-session simple averages.

Down reverses those comparisons. When they disagree, the yardstick is mixed.

The accompanying Bitcoin chart shows those two averages through TradingView's own study. It illustrates the method; it is not the historical dataset used for the counts.

Bitstamp supplies the chart's prices through TradingView. The table uses its own named daily history, and the chart's original timestamp dates this separate illustration.

## Risk, costs and the sequence of a trade

R is the entry-to-stop distance, fixed when a hypothetical trade starts. It lets us compare results without publishing money, account size or trade volume.

A normal day is the average true daily range over 14 sessions. That range includes gaps from the preceding close.

This table deducts 0.05 normal day per completed trade. It is a fixed test assumption, rather than a reconstruction of historical dealing costs.

The rule is evaluated after a completed daily close. A qualifying trade enters at the following open.

If a daily bar touches both stop and target, the test takes the stop first. Daily bars cannot tell us the order of those intraday touches.

A later opening gap is handled before that day's high and low. A trailing stop tightens only after a completed close, for the next bar.

## A long record does not describe a short trade

Some behaviours contain both long and short trades. Their pooled figure is labelled **both sides together**.

A statement about one side uses that side's own count and result. A profitable pooled row cannot establish that its short side paid.

We also compare each trend row with **always long**, using the same stop and holding rule. In gold and the S&P 500, always long paid as much as being long with the trend.

Gold, shares and Bitcoin rose over much of this decade. A rule can inherit that rise without contributing much beyond it.

## The limits that stay beside the numbers

**The search itself matters.** Looking across 54 rows creates opportunities for attractive results by chance.

The independent audit tested that problem and compared choices made on the earlier half with results in the later half. Individual winners looked less convincing after allowing for the full search.

**The calendars differ.** Bitcoin trades through weekends; other markets do not. The same session count can cover a different amount of calendar time.

**The source is a proxy.** Continuous futures histories can contain contract changes. Daily bars also conceal the path within a session.

**The euro and yen figures wait.** Their daily closes often described the start of the day.

We publish no euro or yen result until the repair reaches the live table and its matching side split.

**Construction comparisons mix directions.** The stop-distance, entry and exit comparisons describe both sides together. They are not evidence for a particular long or short call.

## What would change our view

A different cost assumption, repaired source history or a later independent sample can change a rule's apparent advantage. We name the table used so those changes remain visible.

So what: a historical count is a starting comparison. Its direction, denominator, cost and benchmark determine how much weight it deserves.
