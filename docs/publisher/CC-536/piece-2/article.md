# Following the trend worked unevenly

The long and short records tell different stories once the always-long comparison enters the picture.

We see a stronger historical record for following rising markets than for shorting falling ones.
The useful question is whether the trend condition improved on simply taking the long side whenever the rule was free.

Our original table covered six markets, but euro and yen results still await a repair to their daily closes.
We leave those figures out here.
The usable comparison covers gold, oil, the S&P 500 and Bitcoin.

These are hypothetical historical tests, rather than our trade results.
The table comes from Capital Chronicle's audited research; the [method page](https://capitalchronicle.substack.com/p/how-we-measure-a-trading-rule-before) explains its limits.

## What the trend rule actually did

The rule held a position for at most 20 sessions, with a trailing stop and no fixed target.
A long entry required price above its levels 20 and 60 sessions earlier.
Price also had to stand above its 20-session and 50-session simple averages.
The short rule reversed all those conditions.
This was more demanding than an average crossing another average.

The decision came after the close, with entry at the next open.
The initial stop was 1.5 normal days away.
A normal day was the average true daily range over 14 sessions.
The assumed round-trip cost was 0.05 normal day.
R means the initial stop distance; the reported means include that assumed cost.

The always-long comparison used the same stop, trailing rule and maximum holding period, entering whenever free.
It had different entry dates and more trades than the conditional rule.
It was not passive buy-and-hold.

## The long side, with its benchmark beside it

| Market and rule | Trades | Mean net R per trade |
|---|---:|---:|
| Gold futures: long with the trend | 76 | 0.391 |
| Gold futures: always long | 119 | 0.276 |
| Oil futures: long with the trend | 57 | 0.167 |
| Oil futures: always long | 119 | 0.022 |
| S&P 500 futures: long with the trend | 87 | 0.113 |
| S&P 500 futures: always long | 119 | 0.196 |
| Bitcoin: long with the trend | 96 | 0.801 |
| Bitcoin: always long | 176 | 0.56 |

The gold, oil and S&P futures samples run from 2016-11-14 through 2026-11-10.
The Bitcoin sample runs from 2016-11-12 through 2026-11-10, including weekends.
Each mean belongs to its own trade count and that market's stated period.

Gold and Bitcoin had positive long-side means in both halves.
Oil also had positive halves, but its statistical evidence was weaker.
The S&P long-side mean turned negative in the later half.

That last result matters more than a reassuring full-period average.
The S&P trend condition earned less per trade than the always-long comparison over the whole sample.
Gold's conditional mean was higher, but its always-long comparison also paid in both halves.
We cannot attribute all the gold result to the trend test.

## Falling markets were a different proposition

| Market and rule | Trades | Mean net R per trade |
|---|---:|---:|
| Gold futures: short with the trend | 41 | -0.197 |
| Oil futures: short with the trend | 55 | -0.096 |
| S&P 500 futures: short with the trend | 30 | -0.3 |
| Bitcoin: short with the trend | 73 | 0.297 |

Bitcoin's short-side mean was -0.07R in the later half.
Its positive full-period mean therefore did not survive both halves.
Gold and oil shorts also changed from positive early halves to negative later halves.
S&P shorts lost in both halves.

These short results use the same market periods stated above.
They are each side's own record, never a pooled long-and-short figure.

## What this record cannot promise

The original table examined 54 market-and-behaviour rows.
Looking across many rows makes some attractive results likely by chance.
The decade also favoured rising gold, shares and Bitcoin.
The always-long comparison makes that drift visible, without establishing that any difference will persist.

Daily bars leave the order of intraday moves uncertain.
Continuous futures carry contract-roll gaps, and the uniform cost assumption is not a history of actual fills.
Repeated market conditions also make the trades dependent.
Neither the counts nor the average R is a probability for the next trade.

So what: treat the long and short rules separately, and keep the always-long comparison beside every claim about trend skill.
The record supports further testing, with the later-half failures kept in view.
It supplies no current entry signal.
