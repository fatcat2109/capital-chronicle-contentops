# US crude stocks kept falling, but the latest draw slowed

The EIA's October 21, 2026 release records three weekly declines in US commercial crude stocks. A smaller latest draw complicates the reading of a lower mid-October inventory cushion.

US commercial crude stocks stood at about 412.5 million barrels in the week ending October 16, their third consecutive weekly decline, according to [the EIA's October 21, 2026 release](https://www.eia.gov/dnav/pet/hist/LeafHandler.ashx?n=PET&s=WCESTUS1&f=W). The cushion was below the nearby 2025 week, but the latest draw was smaller than the preceding one.

## October changed the stock cushion

The comparison with last autumn flipped during October. The 2026 observations were above the nearby 2025 weeks in late September and at the first October reading. They were below at the next two October readings, ending at about 412.5 million barrels versus about 422.8 million for the week ending October 17, 2025.

The paired week endings are offset by one day on the calendar, with both exact dates shown in the chart. These nearby autumn weeks provide a reference point, not a seasonal normal or an inventory-adequacy threshold.

## The latest draw was smaller

Calculated from EIA's levels, stocks declined by about 3.2 million barrels in the week ending October 2, 8.6 million in the week ending October 9, and 3.1 million in the week ending October 16. The final draw followed a much larger decline.

The smaller latest draw is the counterweight to an accelerating-tightness story. A stockpile can keep shrinking even while its rate of depletion slows. The October 16 observation extends the sequence of draws without making them progressively larger.

## A stock draw does not identify its cause

A draw means fewer barrels remain in commercial storage at week's end. It does not reveal which flow changed: refinery use, imports, exports or production could each alter the stock balance. Without those flows, the inventory series cannot distinguish stronger consumption from other explanations.

A claim about oil prices would need its own evidence: what the market expected, the relevant contract and timing, and what other news arrived. The stock figures alone cannot supply a price surprise or explain an intraday move. They also cover US crude outside the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, rather than the full global oil balance.

## The next test is the balance behind the barrels

EIA listed October 28 as the next release date. Would another draw extend the run, or would a build interrupt it? More useful than the direction alone is the balance behind it: what did refinery use, imports, exports and production contribute? Those answers would test whether the smaller commercial cushion is proving persistent, and why.
