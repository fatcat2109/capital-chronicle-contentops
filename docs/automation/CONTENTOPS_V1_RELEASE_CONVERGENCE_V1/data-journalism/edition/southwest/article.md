# Southwest Airlines' earnings improved without a traffic rebound

Southwest's October 21 results show revenue per seat mile outpacing all-in costs despite a heavier fuel bill. Asset-sale gains complicate the claim that the stronger earnings will endure.

Southwest Airlines reported a stronger third quarter without carrying more passenger traffic. In its [October 21 results](https://investors.southwest.com/news-events/press-releases/detail/1947/southwest-airlines-reports-third-quarter-2026-results-record-revenue-performance-drives-year-over-year-margin-expansion), operating revenue rose 21.2% to $8.4 billion while passenger miles slipped 0.4%. Revenue per available seat mile increased faster than all-in operating cost per seat mile, showing how reported margins could improve despite the fuel headwind.

That supports a narrower conclusion than a demand-led turnaround. Southwest reported a 4.5% operating margin, versus 0.5% a year earlier, but also disclosed $214 million in gains from asset sales and sale-leasebacks. The question is how much of the stronger performance can persist without relying on similar transactions.

## Revenue grew without a traffic expansion

Revenue passengers fell 3.2% year on year, while passenger miles and available seat miles each declined 0.4%. Passenger miles measure traffic over distance; available seat miles measure the capacity offered. Reported load factor, the proportion of that capacity used by paying passengers, was unchanged at 79.8%.

Average passenger fare was $231.69, up from $182.56, a 26.9% increase. More revenue from each passenger is a different result from attracting more traffic. The tables do not isolate the effects of pricing, passenger mix and the company's commercial initiatives.

## More revenue per seat mile cleared higher costs

Revenue per available seat mile, or RASM, reached 18.55 cents, compared with 15.25 cents a year earlier. Total operating expense per available seat mile, or CASM, rose to 17.72 cents from 15.17 cents. Comparing revenue and expense on the same seat-mile base makes the operating relationship clearer than comparing average fare with total fuel spending.

Southwest's revenue per seat mile rose faster than all-in cost per seat mile. That supports the reported margin improvement despite higher fuel costs; it does not establish that every source of the gain will recur.

The company's CASM-X measure rose 3.8%, but it excludes fuel, special items and profit sharing. It cannot stand in for the total cost of flying a seat mile. Nor is the gap between RASM and CASM a measure of net profit or cash flow.

## A fuel-price shock, not more gallons

Southwest's fuel cost per gallon, including tax, rose to $3.92 from $2.40, while gallons consumed fell to 548 million from 554 million. Fuel expense and related taxes increased 61.7%. The higher bill therefore came with lower consumption, rather than a larger volume of fuel burned.

The reported cost per gallon is not a pure spot jet-fuel price: it includes taxes and contract-related costs. The expense, cost-rate and consumption changes are separate comparisons, not additive contributions or a complete explanation of the annual earnings outlook.

## Asset transactions complicate the durability claim

Southwest reported sale gains totalling $214 million. Of that, $118 million came from sale-leasebacks; another $96 million came mainly from sales of aircraft being retired and of engines. Those gains raise a different durability question from what passengers paid to fly. Subtracting them mechanically would not produce a complete measure of recurring earnings.

Southwest's wider unit-revenue advantage challenges a failed-turnaround account. But its release also discloses asset-sale gains, so a stronger reported margin alone does not establish stronger recurring earnings.

Management described demand as strong and its earnings power as durable. The operating tables make a narrower case: higher revenue production and reported margins, alongside fewer passengers. They do not independently establish management's causal explanation or a continuing trend.

## The next quarter must carry its own proof

For the fourth quarter, Southwest forecast year-on-year RASM growth of 19% to 21% and CASM-X growth of 4.0% to 4.5%. Those rates cover different things: CASM-X excludes fuel. The outlook used the October 15 forward fuel curve and assumed the fare environment and demand trends would remain broadly intact.

The same release projected full-year adjusted earnings of $2.60 to $2.85 a share. That issuer forecast is separate from completed-quarter GAAP results and cannot establish how much of the quarter's profit improvement will recur.

Can Southwest keep revenue per seat mile ahead of total cost as fuel conditions change? Later results showing that advantage without greater reliance on asset-sale gains would strengthen the durability case.
