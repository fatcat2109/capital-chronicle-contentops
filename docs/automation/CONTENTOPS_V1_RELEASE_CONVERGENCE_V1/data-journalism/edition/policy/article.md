# A steady federal funds median, a changing pattern of trading

The New York Fed's data show EFFR at 3.88% on all 25 reported business days from September 17 to October 22, 2026. Transaction-rate percentiles and dollar volume varied, giving a fuller picture of overnight funding than the median alone.

The effective federal funds rate was 3.88% on every one of the 25 reported business days from September 17 through October 22, 2026. Yet the [New York Fed's transaction data](https://www.newyorkfed.org/markets/reference-rates/effr) show changes beneath that steady median: the rates near the edges of reported trading moved, and the amount traded varied. Reading the median alone misses both.

That is a useful check on monetary policy implementation, provided the question stays precise. A stable overnight median inside the reported target range describes one part of funding conditions. It does not mean that all transactions occurred at the same rate, or establish what banks paid depositors and charged borrowers.

## The target is an instruction; EFFR is a transaction median

The FOMC establishes the federal funds target; the New York Fed measures reported overnight trading. Its table shows a 3.75%-4.00% target range alongside the 3.88% EFFR throughout these observations. The target and the measured rate are different objects: one states the policy objective, while the other summarizes transactions.

EFFR is a median weighted by transaction volume, rather than a simple average of quoted rates. It covers reported unsecured overnight dollar borrowing by depository institutions from other institutions and certain other lenders. It is an observed reference rate for that market, not a forecast of the next policy decision.

## A steady median, a changing transaction range

On October 14, the reported 1st-percentile transaction rate was 3.80%, compared with 3.85% on October 13; EFFR was 3.88% on both days. At the other end, the 99th-percentile rate was 3.94% on October 21, against 3.89% on October 19, again with no change in the median. These are changes in the reported transaction-rate percentiles, not changes in the policy target.

A median can stay fixed while transactions away from its center change price. Here, the steady EFFR and the moving percentile endpoints describe different features of the same reported market; neither substitutes for the other. Reading them together gives a more useful account of overnight funding than treating the median as the rate on every trade.

The first chart expresses the 1st- and 99th-percentile rates as differences from each day's EFFR, in basis points. One basis point is 0.01 percentage point. Its zero line therefore represents the daily median, not a zero interest rate. The intervals describe transaction percentiles; they exclude the more extreme transactions and are neither the FOMC target range nor a forecast band.

## The amount traded also moved

Reported volume was $83 billion on September 30, between $111 billion on September 29 and $120 billion on October 1. It was $121 billion on October 5 and $119 billion on October 22. EFFR was 3.88% on each of those dates. The second chart shows the amount traded on a separate dollar scale, making the variation visible without implying a relationship between volume and rate changes.

These are daily amounts of reported federal funds trading, not a stock of bank reserves or a count of transactions. The September 30 observation invites a question about what changed, but the table does not identify the participants or causes. Its date alone cannot establish a month-end effect, a funding shortage or a change in banks' willingness to lend.

## What this can establish-and what needs another observation

The strongest countercase to reading these variations as a warning is the steady median itself: EFFR remained within the reported policy target on every observed date. Changes in the transaction-rate tails and reported volume do not, by themselves, establish funding stress or failed policy implementation. A narrow implementation measure can be stable while other features of trading vary.

Capital Chronicle's interpretation is that these observations answer a narrower question than whether monetary policy reached households, companies or asset prices. They document reported overnight funding. A claim about deposit or loan rates needs matching bank-rate evidence; a claim about the next FOMC decision needs evidence about that decision or expectations, rather than an extrapolation from a flat EFFR.

The next useful observation is whether the median moves within or outside the target range reported for the same date, and whether a change in the transaction-rate percentiles persists across several business days. Wider transmission remains a separate test: compare dated deposit and loan rates before drawing conclusions about bank customers.

## Sources and data notes
