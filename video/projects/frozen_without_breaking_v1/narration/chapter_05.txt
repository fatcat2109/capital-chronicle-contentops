Because the economy is doing more with almost no additional labor time.

In BLS’s preliminary second-quarter estimates, nonfarm-business output was 2.5 percent higher than a year earlier. Labor hours were only 0.2 percent higher. Productivity rose 2.2 percent. Unit labor costs rose just 1.4 percent.

That gap is the arithmetic at the center of this film. Aggregate output can grow without total labor hours growing at the same pace.

The data tell us what happened. They do not tell us that artificial intelligence caused it.

Productivity is measured output divided by measured hours. It does not arrive with a label naming the machine, investment, industry mix, workforce change, cyclical decision, or measurement issue behind it.

And demand had not disappeared.

Real GDP grew at a 1.5 percent annual rate in the second quarter. More important for the underlying private economy, real final sales to private domestic purchasers—consumer spending plus private fixed investment—grew at a 3.9 percent rate.

This was not a broad private-demand contraction wearing a strong headline. Consumers and businesses were still spending and investing.

That is the split: the economy can keep buying, investing, and producing while creating very little net employment.

When output per hour rises, compensation can grow without unit labor costs rising as quickly. That may support margins. But aggregate efficiency is not the same thing as broad opportunity.

The economy’s resilience is real.

The next question is who gets to feel it.
