Nine days later, the public record changed.

Payroll employment fell by twenty-three thousand in July.

And unemployment fell too—to 4.1 percent.

Jobs down. Unemployment down. Two official headlines, from two surveys, moving in the same direction when intuition says they should move apart.

Then the past moved.

May’s payroll gain was first reported at one hundred seventy-two thousand. By August, it had become sixty-three thousand. In the July Employment Situation release alone, May moved from one hundred twenty-nine thousand to sixty-three thousand, and June from fifty-seven thousand to twenty thousand—a combined downward revision of one hundred three thousand relative to the estimates available immediately before the release.

With the current estimates, payroll growth averaged just twenty thousand a month from May through July. Over the previous twelve months, the average was thirty-four thousand.

One weak print cannot carry this story alone. The Bureau of Labor Statistics says a one-month payroll change needs to be roughly one hundred twenty-two thousand to clear its usual ninety-percent significance threshold. Minus twenty-three thousand does not.

And on August twenty-eighth, a preliminary benchmark against unemployment-insurance tax records is scheduled to give the payroll level another test.

So this is not a film about pretending one estimate is exact. It is about what happens when revisions, participation, labor flows, and sector data begin pointing toward the same condition.

Which number is telling the truth?
