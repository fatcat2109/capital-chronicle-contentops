Put the dates back in order.

On July twenty-ninth, the Federal Reserve held its policy rate between 3.5 and 3.75 percent. The vote was nine to three. Three policymakers preferred a quarter-point increase.

The July jobs report and its revisions arrived on August seventh. July inflation arrived on August twelfth. July retail sales arrived on August fourteenth.

The July twenty-ninth assessment could not incorporate those releases. They did not yet exist in the public record.

That is a judgment about how the two mandates fit together.

The later data make the policy choice harder, not the principle simpler.

Hiring is weak enough to raise employment risk. Headline inflation at 3.4 percent is high enough to resist an automatic easing story. Second-quarter private demand at a 3.9 percent annualized rate, and year-over-year productivity growth at 2.2 percent, complicate both.

Nothing in those official figures tells us the next rate decision, the next market move, or the date of a recession.

The useful question is what would change the diagnosis.

If payroll growth stays near zero after revisions, hires and quits remain low, claims remain contained, participation fails to recover, and health care continues to dominate net job growth, the freeze has persisted.

If payroll gains broaden, household employment and participation recover, and hires and quits rise without more layoffs, the market is thawing.

If claims and layoffs jump, and unemployment rises alongside job loss rather than labor-force contraction, the diagnosis has expired.

Stasis has become a break.

The evidence available now does not tell us which path comes next.

It tells us what sits beneath the 4.1 percent headline: fewer entrances, fewer voluntary exits, fewer dismissals, narrower sources of growth—and an economy learning, at least for now, to produce more without adding much labor.

The headline says the labor market has not broken.

But a system with so little motion has less room to absorb whatever comes next.
