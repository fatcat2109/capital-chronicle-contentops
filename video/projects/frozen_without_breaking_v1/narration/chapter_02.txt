The payroll number comes from employers and counts jobs. A person with two payroll jobs can appear twice.

The unemployment rate comes from households and classifies people. A person counts once.

In July, that household survey did not find an employment boom. It estimated that employment fell by eighty-seven thousand. The labor force fell by two hundred sixty-four thousand. And the number classified as unemployed fell by one hundred seventy-eight thousand.

The number outside the labor force rose by three hundred eighty-one thousand.

That is how the unemployment rate can fall while both employment and the labor force also fall. The 4.1 percent rate is real under the standard definition. But it is not, by itself, proof that the market strengthened.

Since January, labor-force participation has fallen seven-tenths of a percentage point. The share of the population employed has fallen half a point.

There is a second disagreement beneath the surface. From February through July, payroll employment rose by four hundred twenty-two thousand. A BLS household series adjusted toward payroll-survey concepts fell by five hundred sixty-three thousand.

That does not make either survey a lie. The household survey is smaller and noisier. The surveys count different things. And BLS introduced new population controls in January, so the December-to-January break in household levels must not be presented as real economic movement. Beginning in February avoids that break; it does not erase the uncertainty.

The honest conclusion is not that 264,000 unemployed people “gave up.” The data do not tell us that. The honest conclusion is narrower—and more interesting.

The unemployment rate fell without more household employment.

This market is not simply shedding jobs.

It is losing motion.
