## 1. Nine Days Later

*[BLACK. Authentic microphone and room tone. Use `FED_20260729_LABOR_BASELINE` in full. On screen: `JULY 29, 2026 — FEDERAL RESERVE`.]*

> **CHAIRMAN KEVIN WARSH:** The economy is showing impressive resilience. Even with recent shocks, the trends are positive and reveal solid growth. Job gains have kept pace with the workforce, and the unemployment rate has changed little.

*[Half a beat of black. On screen: `NINE DAYS LATER`.]*

Nine days later, the public record changed.

Payroll employment fell by twenty-three thousand in July.

And unemployment fell too—to 4.1 percent.

Jobs down. Unemployment down. Two official headlines, from two surveys, moving in the same direction when intuition says they should move apart.

Then the past moved.

May’s payroll gain was first reported at one hundred seventy-two thousand. By August, it had become sixty-three thousand. In the July Employment Situation release alone, May moved from one hundred twenty-nine thousand to sixty-three thousand, and June from fifty-seven thousand to twenty thousand—a combined downward revision of one hundred three thousand relative to the estimates available immediately before the release.

With the current estimates, payroll growth averaged just twenty thousand a month from May through July. Over the previous twelve months, the average was thirty-four thousand.

One weak print cannot carry this story alone. The Bureau of Labor Statistics says a one-month payroll change needs to be roughly one hundred twenty-two thousand to clear its usual ninety-percent significance threshold. Minus twenty-three thousand does not.

And on August twenty-eighth, a preliminary benchmark against unemployment-insurance tax records is scheduled to give the payroll level another test.

So this is not a film about pretending one estimate is exact. It is about what happens when revisions, participation, labor flows, and sector data begin pointing toward the same condition.

Which number is telling the truth?

They can all be true.

## 2. The Rate That Fell Without Hiring

The payroll number comes from employers and counts jobs. A person with two payroll jobs can appear twice.

The unemployment rate comes from households and classifies people. A person counts once.

In July, that household survey did not find an employment boom. It estimated that employment fell by eighty-seven thousand. The labor force fell by two hundred sixty-four thousand. And the number classified as unemployed fell by one hundred seventy-eight thousand.

The number outside the labor force rose by three hundred eighty-one thousand.

That is how the unemployment rate can fall while both employment and the labor force also fall. The 4.1 percent rate is real under the standard definition. But it is not, by itself, proof that the market strengthened.

Since January, labor-force participation has fallen seven-tenths of a percentage point. The share of the population employed has fallen half a point.

There is a second disagreement beneath the surface. From February through July, payroll employment rose by four hundred twenty-two thousand. A BLS household series adjusted toward payroll-survey concepts fell by five hundred sixty-three thousand.

That does not make either survey a lie. The household survey is smaller and noisier. The surveys count different things. And BLS introduced new population controls in January, so the December-to-January break in household levels must not be presented as real economic movement. Beginning in February avoids that break; it does not erase the uncertainty.

The honest conclusion is not that 264,000 unemployed people “gave up.” The data do not tell us that. The honest conclusion is narrower—and more interesting.

The unemployment rate fell without more household employment.

This market is not simply shedding jobs.

It is losing motion.

## 3. The Revolving Door Stopped

To see that motion disappear, stop looking at how many jobs exist for a moment. Look at how people move through them.

In June, employers reported 7.359 million openings. But they made only 5.348 million hires. The hiring rate was 3.4 percent, below its 3.9 percent level in February 2020.

There was still about 1.04 opening for every unemployed person. But that ratio is a map, not a matching service. An opening can be in the wrong place, require a different skill, offer the wrong pay, or never become a hire.

From a distance, the market can look balanced. Up close, the door can still be narrow.

Workers were not moving much either. The quit rate was 2.0 percent, below its February 2020 level of 2.3 percent. Quitting is imperfect, but it captures something a payroll total cannot: the willingness and ability to leave one job for another.

And layoffs and discharges? Their rate was 1.1 percent—also below February 2020.

The high-frequency claims data tell the same restrained story. Initial unemployment-insurance claims were two hundred nine thousand in the week ending August eighth, lower than the comparable week a year earlier. Continued claims were lower too.

There is no broad insured-layoff wave in those figures.

That does not make the market painless. Roughly one quarter of unemployed Americans had already been without work for at least twenty-seven weeks in July. Weak entry can create lasting hardship without a spectacular spike in firing.

This is why the best description is low hire, low quit, low fire.

If you already have a job, low firing can feel stable. If you are trying to enter, re-enter, or trade up, low hiring and low quitting can feel like a locked room.

The trapdoor has not opened.

The revolving door has nearly stopped.

And that makes the next question unavoidable: where is the remaining job growth coming from?

## 4. One Sector Holds the Ceiling

Health care added about twenty-two thousand payroll jobs in July.

Over the year, it added roughly three hundred ninety-two thousand.

Total payroll employment across the entire economy rose by only three hundred sixteen thousand.

Subtract health care, and every other sector combined employed about seventy-six thousand fewer people than a year earlier.

That is a remarkable concentration. It is also easy to misuse.

Health care was not the only industry growing. Construction, professional services, and leisure and hospitality were positive over the year. The private-sector diffusion index was 51.8 in July—just above the neutral line of fifty.

So breadth was close to balanced. Magnitude was not. Many small gains can coexist with a few large losses and one dominant source of growth.

The pressure points were visible. Retail lost about nineteen thousand jobs in July. Financial activities lost fourteen thousand and stood one hundred twenty-one thousand below their May 2025 peak. Local government education lost roughly fifty thousand.

Those figures do not prove that consumers have broken, credit has seized up, or public services are in structural retreat. Employment is not a balance sheet.

But a labor market relying on one socially essential sector for more than all of its net annual growth is not broadly dynamic. It is load-bearing.

And concentration makes a calm headline more fragile than it looks.

So why has an economy with so little labor absorption not stopped?

## 5. The Machine Keeps Running

Because the economy is doing more with almost no additional labor time.

In BLS’s preliminary second-quarter estimates, nonfarm-business output was 2.5 percent higher than a year earlier. Labor hours were only 0.2 percent higher. Productivity rose 2.2 percent. Unit labor costs rose just 1.4 percent.

That gap is the arithmetic at the center of this film. Aggregate output can grow without total labor hours growing at the same pace.

The data tell us what happened. They do not tell us that artificial intelligence caused it.

Productivity is measured output divided by measured hours. It does not arrive with a label naming the machine, investment, industry mix, workforce change, cyclical decision, or measurement issue behind it.

And demand had not disappeared.

Real GDP grew at a 1.5 percent annual rate in the second quarter. More important for the underlying private economy, real final sales to private domestic purchasers—consumer spending plus private fixed investment—grew at a 3.9 percent rate.

This was not a broad private-demand contraction wearing a strong headline. Consumers and businesses were still spending and investing.

That is the split: the economy can keep buying, investing, and producing while creating very little net employment.

When output per hour rises, compensation can grow without unit labor costs rising as quickly. That may support margins. But aggregate efficiency is not the same thing as broad opportunity.

The economy’s resilience is real.

The next question is who gets to feel it.

## 6. The Missing Share

Average hourly earnings rose 3.2 percent over the year.

A different measure—the Employment Cost Index—showed private wages up 3.1 percent. Because it controls more carefully for shifts in job and industry mix, that agreement corroborates the roughly 3 percent wage reading.

But July consumer prices were 3.4 percent higher than a year earlier. Real average hourly earnings were down 0.2 percent. Employers’ private-industry health-insurance benefit costs were up 6 percent.

Workers were not experiencing a broad real-pay boom.

Inflation was not one clean number either. Core consumer prices, excluding food and energy, were up 2.5 percent. Energy helped push the headline higher.

Then there is labor’s share of the output being produced. In the nonfarm business sector, BLS’s preliminary estimate fell to 52.9 percent—the lowest reading in a series beginning in 1947.

That number is striking. It is also preliminary and vulnerable to revision. It cannot tell us why the share moved, and it should not carry the thesis by itself.

For now, the defensible conclusion is narrower. The economy has recently produced more without needing many more labor hours. Workers have fewer completed job moves. Real hourly pay has slipped slightly. And low quits may reduce the leverage that comes from being able to leave.

This is the uncomfortable version of productivity.

An economy can become more efficient before it becomes more generous.

And that split now reaches the institution asked to balance employment and prices.

## 7. No Easy Exit

Put the dates back in order.

On July twenty-ninth, the Federal Reserve held its policy rate between 3.5 and 3.75 percent. The vote was nine to three. Three policymakers preferred a quarter-point increase.

The July jobs report and its revisions arrived on August seventh. July inflation arrived on August twelfth. July retail sales arrived on August fourteenth.

The July twenty-ninth assessment could not incorporate those releases. They did not yet exist in the public record.

*[J-cut authentic room tone. Use local `FED_20260729_DUAL_MANDATE` approximately `00:03.55–00:19.35`.]*

> **CHAIRMAN KEVIN WARSH:** I don't believe that either part of our mandate is generally at war with the other part. I do not believe that price stability and full employment is an either-or proposition.

That is a judgment about how the two mandates fit together.

The later data make the policy choice harder, not the principle simpler.

Hiring is weak enough to raise employment risk. Headline inflation at 3.4 percent is high enough to resist an automatic easing story. Second-quarter private demand at a 3.9 percent annualized rate, and year-over-year productivity growth at 2.2 percent, complicate both.

Nothing in those official figures tells us the next rate decision, the next market move, or the date of a recession.

The useful question is what would change the diagnosis.

If payroll growth stays near zero after revisions, hires and quits remain low, claims remain contained, participation fails to recover, and health care continues to dominate net job growth, the freeze has persisted.

If payroll gains broaden, household employment and participation recover, and hires and quits rise without more layoffs, the market is thawing.

If claims and layoffs jump, and unemployment rises alongside job loss rather than labor-force contraction, the diagnosis has expired.

Stasis has become a break.

The evidence available now does not tell us which path comes next.

It tells us what sits beneath the 4.1 percent headline: fewer entrances, fewer voluntary exits, fewer dismissals, narrower sources of growth—and an economy learning, at least for now, to produce more without adding much labor.

The headline says the labor market has not broken.

But a system with so little motion has less room to absorb whatever comes next.
